Alice, Bob, and Eve

Alice sends Bob a string of qubits, each in a randomly chosen basis carrying a random bit. Bob measures each one in his own randomly chosen basis. Afterward, they only keep the bits where their bases happened to match — and if Eve was listening in, that alone leaves a fingerprint they can measure.

Eavesdropper

Eve intercepts every qubit Measures in a random basis, then resends what she got — the only thing she can do without a copying machine quantum mechanics forbids.
What you're seeing: each column is one qubit: Alice's bit and basis at top, Eve's intercepted guess in the middle when she's listening, Bob's basis and result at the bottom. Columns where Alice and Bob's bases matched are kept (highlighted); the rest are simply discarded — publicly announcing your basis choice, never your bit, leaks nothing useful. Without Eve, every kept bit matches exactly. With Eve intercepting and resending, she gets the wrong basis half the time, and each time she does, Bob's result becomes a coin flip even when his basis matched Alice's — producing a predictable 25% error rate in the kept bits. That number, checked against a small public sample of the key, is how Alice and Bob catch her without ever meeting in person.
