Why Quarks Never Travel Alone

Pull two quarks apart and the strong field between them doesn't thin out the way gravity or electromagnetism would — it stretches like a stubborn rubber band, storing more energy the farther they go. Keep pulling, and nature doesn't hand you a free quark. It hands you a new quark-antiquark pair instead.

What you're seeing: gravity and electromagnetism both weaken with distance, following an inverse-square law — but the strong force does the opposite. In this simplified flux-tube picture, the energy stored between a quark and antiquark grows roughly in proportion to how far apart you pull them, like a rubber band that never gets easier to stretch. Keep pulling, and eventually it becomes energetically favorable to create a new quark-antiquark pair out of the vacuum rather than continue stretching the tube. The tube snaps into two shorter tubes, each one now a complete, color-neutral meson of its own. You do not get one naked quark. You get more hadrons — nature responds to your attempt to liberate a quark by giving you additional particles instead.
