Feynman's Glass Trick

A single glass surface reflects about 4% of the light hitting it — simple enough. But a full pane of glass has two surfaces, and as you change its thickness by a fraction of a wavelength, the reflection swings smoothly between roughly 0% and 16%. Feynman explained it with a tiny spinning arrow for every possible path — add them nose to tail, then square the result.

What you're seeing: two paths lead to the same event — reflecting off the front surface, or entering the glass and reflecting off the back surface instead. Feynman's method gives each path a tiny arrow whose length reflects roughly 4% single-surface reflection and whose direction spins as the photon travels — a longer path means more spinning. Add the two arrows head to tail to get one final arrow, then square its length: that's the reflection probability. Change the thickness by a fraction of a wavelength and the second arrow's spin shifts, swinging the sum between full cancellation (≈0%, the arrows point opposite ways) and full reinforcement (≈16%, they point the same way). Firing individual photons shows the same randomness as the double-slit experiment back in Chapter 1: no single photon's fate is predictable, but the fraction that reflect matches this calculated curve exactly.
